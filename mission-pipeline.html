Mapped Missions v2 · the whole path

From raster to mission

A species distribution model outputs a probability surface. A player opens the map and sees a dozen pins, each with a grade. This is everything that happens in between — how a peak is found, how it is scored, how the day's missions are drawn from those scores, and why the grade on the pin is a different number from the score that put it there.

Vocabulary

read this first

Everything below is written in these terms. B1–B8 and R1–R4 are the design document's names for the pipeline stages in the diagram above, and they are used throughout as ordinary nouns.

raster
One species' predicted-suitability surface, served as map tiles. One raster = one species.
p
The model's probability at a pixel, 0.1913–1.0. Below 0.1913 the service returns nothing.
level (z6–z11)
A zoom level of the tile pyramid. Coarse z6 covers ~1.7 km per pixel; fine z11 covers ~52 m.
σ (sigma)
The Gaussian smoothing width, fixed at 4 pixels at every level. Because the pyramid supplies the scale, one constant covers the whole ladder.
blob radius r
r = √2·σ in ground metres — the natural size of a feature at that level, and the mission's size. 296 m at z11, 9.5 km at z6.
valid / censored / absent
The three states a pixel can be in: above the floor; below it (the model asserted low); or never modelled at all.
contrast
How much a peak stands above its own surroundings, measured over a 2σ neighbourhood. The shape statistic.
prominence
Height above the saddle where a peak merges with a higher one. Computed as a diagnostic, deliberately never scored.
candidate
One surviving peak in the catalogue: a species, a level, a coordinate, and its scores. Not yet a mission.
interest
The five-term product in Part II. Decides the order of the draw, and nothing else.
grade
The tier shown on the pin — Routine to Critical. A different formula answering a different question; Part IV.
zone
The polygon around the pin the player has to stand in. A level set of the smoothed surface, rebuilt on demand.

Part I

Finding the peaks

Eight batch stages turn ~1,000 species rasters into a catalogue of scoreable places. Every number quoted in this part is measured on the Thun region build — a 15 km box, 881 native species, levels z6–z11, 9,366 surviving candidates.

Species selection

B1

Which species are even eligible?

The only hard gate in the entire pipeline. Everything downstream is a weight, never a cut.

  1. Enumerate the region's species catalogue → {binomial: raster_id}.
  2. Native only — the model's positive native flag, not "anything not flagged introduced". The two are not complements: measured at Lausanne, 218 species split 116 native / 60 introduced / 42 flagged neither, and that third bucket is the garden catalogue (Paulownia, Nerium, Cercis, Hyacinthus). Species carrying no trait data at all pass rather than being silently dropped.
  3. Poaceae excluded — grasses are not a photographable mission.
  4. Duplicate raster ids collapse: one raster, one species.

This runs once per region rather than once per request, which is what deletes v1's per-request model call and its cache entirely.

Thun — 881 species with a raster inside the box.

Surface preparation

B3

What is actually being measured — and where is there nothing to measure?

Nodata is filled with 0.0, everywhere, at every stage. Detection, connectivity, level sets and shape statistics all run on one surface; a statistic computed on a differently-filled surface than the one its peak was detected on is not a statistic of that peak. So there is no dual-fill scheme and no per-stage exception.

Nodata here means "the model's output fell below 0.1913" — a censored low value, not a missing measurement. Excluding those pixels from any average biases it upward, badly so for narrow-range species. v1 filled with 0.20 instead, purely to protect gradient-based operators from the cliff at the range boundary. v2 uses no gradient operator on the raw surface: peak detection and level sets care only about ordering and connectivity, for which a cliff is harmless. The constraint that forced the 0.20 fill does not exist here.

Three states, not two

A pixel can be empty because the model said low, or because nobody ever asked. Those are opposite claims and a naive mask collapses them into one.

StateMeaningFilled withReal ground?Used for
validTile served, pixel above the 0.1913 floordecoded pyespeaks may sit only here; fragment test; quantiles
censoredTile served, pixel below the floor — the model asserted "low"0.0yescounted in contrast's reference mean
absentOutside the model's domain, or never fetched0.0noexcluded from every reference region

Range edges are signal, not artefact. A peak whose surroundings fall below the floor genuinely is more distinct: the model is asserting the species stops there, and the one patch of suitable ground in an otherwise unsuitable valley is a good mission. No correction is applied to range-edge peaks and none should be — any scheme that "de-biases" them deletes the very signal that makes them worth visiting. What must be handled is the mosaic edge, which is not the range edge: the window is extended by a 12 px (3σ) apron so every candidate inside the region has its full reference annulus over real model output.

Two smoothings of the same surface

This is the subtlest thing in B3 and it exists because of one measured failure.

smoothed = gaussian(filled, σ=4px) ← peaks are found and shaped on this smoothed_nc = gaussian(p·valid) / gaussian(valid) ← probabilities are read from this

The second is a normalised convolution: it averages only real ground. On Asplenium ruta-muraria, whose range covers about 1% of its window, blurring across the 0-fill read 0.008 at peaks whose real probability is 0.40 — a 50× error, scaling with how fragmented the range is, which scrambled the uncertainty term across species. Detection legitimately wants the 0-filled surface (the fill is what makes a range-edge peak sharp); the absolute reading of how sure is the model here must not touch it. Same pixel, two readings, kept apart on purpose.

Peak detection

B4

Where are the local maxima, and which of them mean anything?

  1. Strict local maxima of smoothed, 8-connected, restricted to valid pixels. Probability is quantised to 201 levels, so smoothed plateaus are common: each connected plateau collapses to one point at its centroid, snapped to the nearest actual member for concave shapes. Deterministic, so a rebuild reproduces the catalogue exactly.
  2. The fragment test. Keep a peak only if the connected component of valid pixels containing it has area ≥ 2πσ² ≈ 101 px.
  3. The absolute noise floor on contrast — specified, implemented, and currently switched off. See below.
  4. Record p_peak from smoothed_nc and contrast_raw from the contrast field.

Why the fragment test is not a quality filter

A shape statistic measured on a patch smaller than the operator measuring it reports the patch's existence, not its shape. A three-pixel valid island at p = 0.25 has, at σ = 4 px, a contrast of roughly its full height — clearing any plausible noise floor by an order of magnitude, permanently, however marginal it is. Note that the fraction of valid ground in the annulus cannot detect this: a genuine range-edge peak inside a large range and a peak on a tiny island can have identical valid fractions. The discriminator is the size of the component, nothing else.

Because σ is constant in pixels, the threshold is one level-independent constant. And it removes almost nothing — it re-files: a fragment carrying 25 px at z10 carries 100 px at z11, so a candidate that fails at one level passes one level finer, hosting its mission at the scale where its shape is measurable. It is the cascade's own logic applied to the domain instead of to the surface.

Why the noise floor is measured and reported rather than enforced

The h-maxima transform is an absolute threshold in probability units, and it is the one test that asks whether a peak's height above its surroundings exceeds what noise alone produces. It is implemented in full: phase-randomise the surface's Fourier spectrum — same texture, no features — rerun the identical detect-and-measure pipeline on that surrogate, and take the 99th percentile of its contrast distribution as h. It ships behind H_MAXIMA_ENABLED, off by default, because of what it measures when it is turned on.

On Thun the fitted floor came out at a median of 0.10, against real peak contrasts with a median of 0.025 and a 90th percentile of 0.059 — above the 90th percentile of real peaks, removing 19,394 of 20,121.

That is the surrogate working correctly, not failing. These surfaces are smoothed random fields, so a faithful phase-randomised surrogate resembles the real surface, and the 99th percentile of such a null keeps about 1% of peaks by construction however good they are. The expected magnitude quoted alongside the rule — 0.005–0.02, from s / (2σ√π) — is a white-noise argument, and these surfaces are correlated, which is why the fitted value lands two decades away from it.

So the surrogate is kept as a diagnostic and the gating is done structurally instead, by the fragment test above and the cascade below: both ask "is this a feature at the scale it claims" without needing a threshold in probability units. H_MAXIMA_FIXED is available for a small absolute floor (~0.008) if a build wants one, and h is written to the side table on every build so the question stays auditable after the fact.

The cascade filter

B5

Does this peak survive an octave of blur?

One rule: a peak at level z is kept only if a raw peak exists at level z−1 — one octave coarser — within d = σ_coarse / 2. This is the minimal scale-space persistence criterion. A feature that vanishes when you step back once was never a feature.

  • Each level is tested against the raw coarser set, before that level's own filtering. Removals do not cascade, so every level's filter is independent of every other and the whole stage is embarrassingly parallel.
  • Match on distance to the coarser summit, never on basin containment. Basins approximately partition the plane, so a containment test passes everything.
  • Density gradation survives. Requiring one octave rather than full scale-space lifetime keeps fine peaks far more numerous than coarse ones — which is exactly right: small missions should be dense, large ones sparse.
  • The coarsest level has no coarser neighbour and is kept unconditionally.

The arithmetic: z10 peak density is 0.222/km², so a pure-noise z11 peak lands within d of a coarse peak by accident about 3% of the time at d = σ_coarse/2 = 210 m — roughly a 30× noise reduction. It is a strong filter, not elimination; at looser radii half the surviving fine peaks could still be noise.

Shape statistics

B6

How sharp is this peak, compared to what?

Contrast

contrast = smoothed − ref ref = Σ(w·p·d) / Σ(w·d) over a 2σ box, d = 1 on valid|censored, 0 on absent

The reference is a normalised convolution over the domain mask. censored pixels are included at their fill value of 0.0 — they are the model asserting low suitability, and dropping them would erase the range-edge signal B3 keeps on purpose. absent pixels are excluded because nothing was asserted there.

Normalisation — within species and within level

Contrast is converted to a rank inside its own (species × level) group, and never compared across either.

  • Within species, because with nodata filled at 0 a peak on an isolated range fragment measures its height against the fill, while a peak inside a continuous range measures against real ground. Systematically, fragmented-range species would outrank continuous-range ones on shape alone — double-counting rarity, which is already an interest term, and losing the sharp-peak-in-a-widespread-species case, which is exactly the mission type worth having.
  • Within level, because there are ~16,000× more candidates at the finest level than the coarsest. A pooled distribution is ~99% fine candidates, and a coarse candidate's percentile would be a rank against a population it does not belong to.

The rank uses the plotting position (k+1)/(n+1), strictly inside (0, 1) — not k/(n−1), which assigns exactly 0.0 to the worst candidate of every group. Part II explains why an exact zero is catastrophic rather than merely low. A group of one maps to 0.5, unremarkable rather than penalised, which falls out of the formula with no special case.

Prominence — measured, stored, never scored

Prominence is reference-free, which makes it the obvious candidate to carry an absolute floor. It cannot, for a structural reason: with nodata filled at 0, a peak on an isolated fragment merges only through the fill, so its prominence equals its full height. For those candidates prominence stops being an independent statistic and becomes a copy of p_peak — which is ≥ 0.1913 by construction and so exceeds any plausible noise floor by an order of magnitude. A floor carried by prominence would be structurally incapable of rejecting exactly the candidates most in need of rejecting.

This is not an argument against the 0-fill, and the fragment peak is not mis-measured — it really is the highest thing for miles. It is a statement about what the number means: prominence measures topological isolation, and on a fragmented range that isolation is a property of the domain rather than of the surface. Scoring it would duplicate contrast on a continuous range and duplicate p_peak on a fragmented one. It is computed only behind a flag (it is the build's CPU bottleneck) and is absent from the shipped catalogue.

Retention

Keep the top 20 per species per level by normalised contrast. This is a budget, not a quality bar — it bounds the catalogue and re-ranks nothing.

Delimitation

B7

Where exactly does the player have to stand?

The zone is a level set of the smoothed surface at the peak's own level, seeded at the peak, and the threshold is bisected so the delivered polygon hits the level's own target area, πr² — 27.6 ha at z11, 441 ha at z9. Zones are never stored: they are a deterministic function of (species, level, peak position), rebuilt on demand from the mission id and cached to disk.

  1. Close the mask so the zone is not a kidney with a bite out of it.
  2. Bisect the threshold 20 times on [0, p_peak·0.98], keeping the highest threshold whose closed, peak-containing component still meets the target area — clamped to [0.3×, 3×] that target, per level.
  3. Union with a 60 m disc around the peak, re-take the component, fill holes.
  4. Take the convex hull, then vectorise: edge tracing → Douglas-Peucker → Chaikin ×2 → Douglas-Peucker.

Two decisions worth recording. There is no radial decay. v1 multiplied the field by a Gaussian centred on the pin, which is already at 0.685 by the target radius — a 31% radial gradient that swamps the real variation in a smoothed surface, so every zone came out a circle: a contour of the decay, not of the data. Here the field is left alone and extent is bounded by a hard clip at 2.5r, generous enough that the target area is ~16% of the clip disc. The hull is taken because a raw level set is a blob with inlets and islands, and the client tests its own GPS position against every zone locally — the hull guarantees a single ring with no holes, so that test stays one loop. The threshold is bisected on the hull's area, not the level set's, so hulling does not overshoot the target.

The catalogue

B8

What survives to disk?

Descriptors, not polygons. One row per candidate: species, raster id, level, lat/lon at 5 dp, p_peak, contrast_raw, contrast_norm, the fraction of the reference annulus that was valid, the fragment's area, the global occurrence count, and the five interest terms with their product. Plus a side table with one row per (species × level) holding p_q50 and p_q90 — the two quantiles the species-relative interest term needs — and the candidate counts at every filter stage.

That side table is what makes a species-relative score free at request time: two floats per species-level rather than one per candidate.

mission id = gpn3:<area>:<raster_id>:<level>:<lat_e5>:<lon_e5>

Everything needed to rebuild the zone is in the id, so the detail endpoint is stateless and old ids stay resolvable as long as their catalogue version is retained.

Part II

The interest score

Five terms decide how interesting a candidate is. None of them decides where it goes — placement was settled upstream by B1–B8 before this runs. Keeping those two questions apart is the whole point of the redesign.

interest = modesty(p_peak) × worth(n_global) × stronghold(n_local) × distinct(contrast_norm) × rel_height(p_peak; species)

Multiplicative, each factor roughly 0–1. A product means every term can veto — but none may veto absolutely, which is why each carries a floor above zero. Measured across the Thun catalogue: 9,366 candidates, interest from 1.2×10⁻⁵ to 0.43, median 0.0047.

Model uncertainty

modesty(p_peak)

How sure is the model here?

modesty(p) = exp(−½ · ((p − 0.50) / 0.15)²)

A Gaussian centred where the model is genuinely undecided. It falls away toward 0.9, where the species is a confident stronghold and there is little to learn, and toward 0.2, where the prediction is too faint to find on the ground.

p is used absolutely, never percentile-normalised. A species the model is confident about everywhere should score low here; normalising would falsely promote its best peak to "most uncertain" and destroy the signal.

Species scarcity

worth(n_global)

Is this species worth anyone's walk?

worth(n) = floor + (1 − floor) / (1 + (n / 60000)^1.0)

A logistic in log space over the global occurrence count. It keeps falling well past the point a player would notice: 20k records → 0.75, 200k → 0.24, 1.6M → 0.05. A ubiquitous tree and a locally uncommon herb must not both score zero — that is how Fagus sylvatica came to sit beside Gentiana lutea in v1.

The floor is the entire difference between this term and the gate it replaces. Urtica dioica scores near it, which after the draw exponent is odds of hundreds of thousands to one against — never in practice, without ever being never.

Unverified stronghold

stronghold(n_local)

Is the model extrapolating here, rather than remembering?

stronghold(n) = 0.15 + 0.85 / (1 + n / 10)

High prediction, few or no records nearby. Deliberately a separate term from uncertainty: local occurrence density is sampling effort — biased toward roads, towns and popular trails — and must never be read as model doubt.

The local count comes from one occurrence-facet query per 20 km square, answered for every species at once. That response is truncated, so a species missing from it has fewer records than the cutoff, which is not the same as none. Reading it as zero is the strongest possible rarity signal and is wrong, so unknown maps to 0.65 rather than to 1.0.

Caveat to carry forward: "no records" correlates with "remote", which correlates with "hard to reach".

Peak distinctness

distinct(contrast_norm)

Is this one of the sharpest places for this species?

distinct(c) = 0.01 + 0.99 · c c = rank of contrast within (species × level), as (k + 1) / (n + 1)

Contrast is the peak measured against its own surroundings at 2σ, normalised within the species and within the level and never compared across either — for the reasons B6 gives.

Note what this term honestly answers. "Sharpest" is not "best" — but the ladder absorbs most of the gap, because a broad optimum is never penalised for being broad, it is simply detected as a high-contrast peak at a coarser level, where the zone size follows the level.

The rank uses the plotting position (k+1)/(n+1), not k/(n−1). The latter assigns exactly 0.0 to the worst candidate of every group — see the floors section below.

Placement quality

rel_height(p_peak; species)

Is this a good place for this species?

rel_height = 0.05 + 0.95 · clip((p − q50) / (q90 − q50), 0, 1) q50, q90 = quantiles of p over that species' valid pixels at that level

Every other height signal in the score is either absolute (modesty) or pure shape (distinct). Nothing else asks how a peak compares to this species' own ground. A Gentiana peak at 0.85 is exceptional; a Fagus peak at 0.85 is ordinary. Without this term the score cannot tell them apart.

It saturates instead of scaling. modesty penalises p → 0.9; a linear reward here would pull the other way, and in a product the fight would be settled by whichever curve happened to be steeper — an accident, not a design. Clipping at q90 makes this a floor on placement quality: don't send a player to a peak that is mediocre for its species. It is flat everywhere modesty is doing its work.

The quantiles are taken from smoothed_nc, the same surface p_peak is read from. Taking them from the raw surface compares two different distributions: on Gentiana acaulis (fragmented, alpine) the raw median was 0.445 against a smoothed median of 0.287, so 86% of its peaks fell below the raw median and collapsed onto the floor — while Fagus sylvatica, whose range is continuous, lost only 21%. The bias fell hardest on exactly the alpine species the catalogue exists to surface.

Why every term has a floor

A zero is not a low score — it is a gate

The draw takes log(weight), so a single exact 0.0 anywhere in the product does not make a mission unlikely. It makes it unreachable. That contradicts the design's own rule that nothing after the species filter is a cut: a poor mission must be unlikely rather than impossible.

Two terms produced hard zeros by construction. rel_height clipped at 0 — and q50 is a median, so by definition about half of all candidates landed on or below it. distinct gave exactly 0 to the worst candidate of every group. Together they zeroed 54.9% of the catalogue.

Both now floor above zero, following the pattern WORTH_FLOOR already set. At the floor the odds after the draw exponent are roughly 160,000:1 against — never in practice, without ever being never.

The same trap is one round() away at the storage layer: the score is a product of five sub-unit terms, so a legitimate value can sit at 1e−6 and round(x, 5) would write it back as exactly 0.0, reintroducing by rounding the gate the floors just removed. The catalogue stores interest to six significant digits for that reason.

Part III

How the day's missions are drawn

The catalogue is static. Only the draw turns over. Everything in this part happens per request, in milliseconds, over rows already loaded from disk — no tiles are read, no external service is called.

The query

R1

A spatial lookup for candidates near the viewport, widened by the largest blob radius in play so a mission whose zone reaches the screen is not missed because its peak sits just off it. Levels z8–z11 by default. Expect a few hundred rows.

Measured at the Thun centre, 2 km radius: 544 candidates in range.

The weighted draw

R3

Of everything nearby, which do you get offered today?

weight[i] = interest[i] × scale_weight(level[i]) key[i] = DRAW_EXPONENT · log(weight[i]) + Gumbel(id[i], day) order = sort by key, descending

This is Gumbel-top-k: adding an independent Gumbel-distributed number to each log-weight and sorting is exact weighted sampling without replacement, in one sort and no rejection loop.

DRAW_EXPONENT = 4 multiplies the log, so the whole weight — interest and scale together — is raised to the fourth power. Two consequences follow, and both are visible in what the map serves.

  • Interest separates hard. A candidate sitting at a term floor is not merely unlikely, it is on the order of 10⁵-to-1 against the median.
  • Coarse levels are strongly favoured. scale_weight spans 1 to 1024 across the ladder, and the exponent carries that to 1 : 10¹². Fine candidates outnumber coarse ones by roughly the same order, so the two effects work against each other — the net result on a Thun viewport is a first twenty of 2×z11, 6×z10, 8×z9 and 4×z8, a spread of scales rather than a wall of the finest level.

The noise is a property of the candidate, not of the array

The Gumbel value for each candidate is derived from a hash of day:mission_id, never from its position in the array. A position-based random draw hands out noise by index, so the same peak would get a different value depending on which other candidates happened to share its request — two overlapping viewports would disagree about it, and panning the map would reshuffle missions instead of revealing more of them. Hashing the id makes the accept/reject outcome a property of the ground and the date alone.

The same logic governs the seed itself: daily_seed depends on the day and nothing else. A mission is a property of the ground, so the seed must not know where the player is standing.

Exclusion is derived from scale

Walking the order, a candidate is rejected if it sits too close to one already accepted:

same species: distance < 1.5 · max(r_i, r_j) ← the larger of the two blob radii different species: distance < 50 m ← pins must not overlap visually

This single rule replaces v1's cell quotas, per-cell species caps and fixed 400 m species gap, and it delivers three things those constants could not:

  • Density scales correctly. A 592 m-radius mission permits another of the same species a kilometre away; a 9.5 km one does not.
  • Nested duplicates fall out of the same test, with no separate machinery.
  • Scale variety emerges free. Because the order is seeded per day, some days a coarse candidate wins first and blocks the fine ones beneath it; other days a fine one wins and the coarse one is blocked. The same species appears at a different size on different days.

Then a coverage pass, then a spread

The weighted draw concentrates missions where interest is high, which is correct for ranking and wrong for a map: a player standing in a dull valley should still have somewhere to walk to. Two corrections follow, and neither one re-ranks anything.

  1. Coverage. Any 1.5 km cell that holds candidates but won nothing gets its best one — walking the same seeded order, under the same exclusion rules, so what fills a hole is still the day's best candidate there. A cell stays empty when its only candidates sit inside an accepted mission's zone, which is the right outcome.
  2. Spread. The viewport asks for ~20 missions and the draw usually accepts hundreds. Truncating by rank would put every pin on the best ground in the frame — which is the "nothing to do around here" complaint seen from anywhere else in it. Instead the budget is spent round-robin over coverage cells, visiting cells in the rank order of their best mission. Quality still decides which cell fills first and which mission represents it; the budget is simply spent covering ground before it is spent stacking one cell.

Where the scale balance actually lives

Only one place in the system decides how many coarse missions a viewport gets: the exponentiated scale_weight in this ordering. Nothing upstream compensates for it — B6 normalises contrast within a level precisely so that scale balance is not smuggled into the shape statistics, and the exclusion rule spaces missions apart without preferring a size.

That makes the draw the single knob for it, and a sensitive one: because the exponent applies to the whole weight, halving DRAW_EXPONENT or applying it to interest alone changes the level mix substantially. Applied to interest alone, the same viewport's first twenty becomes 8×z11, 8×z10, 3×z9 and 1×z8, with a median interest of 0.056 against the current 0.039. Both are defensible maps; they are not the same map, and the choice is made here.

R4

Deliberately minimal, because the pin is a visual marker: what actually matters is the zone, and the app lists the missions whose zone contains the player. The pin snaps to the nearest walkable path point to the peak, clamped to stay inside the zone; if no path intersects the zone, it stays on the peak itself. Zones are rebuilt from the mission id on demand and cached to disk, so a nightly warm-up and a live request share the work rather than each paying for it.

Part IV

The grade

The tier on the pin — Mission: Routine Important Major Critical — is not the interest score, is not a rescaling of it, and does not rank the same way. It answers a different question, and the two are kept apart on purpose.

The four tiers are named common, rare, epic and legendary in the API and in params.py; the app shows them as Routine, Important, Major and Critical. This page uses the names the player sees, and gives the internal key where the code is being quoted.

Three questions, three numbers

It is worth stating all three together, because conflating any two of them is the failure mode this separation exists to prevent.

NumberQuestionSettledShapeVisible to the player?

The grade and the points system must never share a formula, or tuning one would silently move the other. The grade and the interest score share inputs — both read the model's confidence and the species' global record count — but combine them differently and to different ends.

Two factors, weighed equally

Each factor is the same question asked of a different source: how much would this observation teach us?

Model uncertainty = 1 − exp(−modesty / 0.35) Rare worldwide = clip( log(200000 / n) / log(200000 / 50), 0, 1 ) score = mean of the factors that could be measured tier = first tier whose floor the score clears

Model uncertainty saturates rather than clips. In v1 its input was an unbounded surface anomaly, and a handful of enormous values would otherwise have compressed every ordinary mission into the bottom of the scale. In v2 the input is modesty — already 0–1 — so the exponential mostly serves to expand the low end: modesty 0.1 → 0.25, modesty 0.5 → 0.76, modesty 1.0 → 0.94. That ceiling of 0.943 is structural: no mission can score higher on this axis.

Rare worldwide is log-scaled, because occurrence counts span six orders of magnitude. Linear in the count is what the observation points system does, and there a species with a thousand records and one with fifty score within a percent of each other. On a log scale they are 0.64 and 1.00. It saturates at both ends: 50 records or fewer is a full 1.0, 200,000 or more is a flat 0.0.

A factor that cannot be measured is left out of the average, never counted as zero. Where the local occurrence facet is truncated that is every mission in the region at once, and counting it as zero once turned a whole viewport Routine.

No single factor can reach the top tier alone. That is the point: a globally rare species the model is confident about, in a well-surveyed spot, is a nice find but not a discovery, and the grade should say so.

The tiers, and why v2 had to recalibrate them

The factors are v1's, unchanged. Only the cut-offs are v2's, and they had to move for a specific reason: v1 fed Model uncertainty an unbounded band-pass anomaly, while v2 feeds it modesty — which is bounded at 1, and which the draw actively selects to be near 1. Missions arriving at the grade are therefore pre-filtered to score high on one of its two axes. On v1's cut-offs, 84.6% of a Thun draw graded Critical; over a 1,200-mission sample it was 96%. A grade that says "Critical" nine times out of ten is not a grade.

What the grade is actually measuring right now

The Rare worldwide axis is flat for most missions. 60% of catalogue rows belong to species with more than 200,000 global records, where that factor saturates at exactly zero — so for the median mission the grade is simply half of Model uncertainty. Across the drawn set, the score correlates with Model uncertainty at 0.94 and with Rare worldwide at 0.30.

And yet scarcity is what promotes. Because Model uncertainty is capped at 0.943 and the draw pushes most missions near that cap, no mission can clear the 0.58 Critical floor on uncertainty alone — the top of that axis contributes at most 0.47. Every Critical mission is a scarce species. The two statements are both true: uncertainty moves the score across its whole range, scarcity decides who reaches the top of it.

Interest and grade agree only loosely — they correlate at 0.33 across the drawn set. That is by design and is the reason both exist: interest asks what is worth offering (and knows about peak sharpness, local sampling effort and placement quality, none of which the grade sees), while the grade asks what would be worth learning. A dense cluster of well-surveyed common species can be a perfectly good walk and still, correctly, grade Routine.

The third axis the grade is built for

The grade's design describes three factors weighed equally — Model uncertainty, Rare worldwide, and Unrecorded nearby: few records in the area, so the prediction here is an extrapolation rather than a memory. The app carries the label for all three; grade.localRarity → "Unrecorded nearby" sits in every translation file, ready for a response that includes it.

What ships today computes two, and the average is taken over what was measured — which is the same rule that already governs a truncated occurrence facet. The third number is not missing from the system, only from the grade: stronghold computes exactly it for the interest score, from the same facet query, and is stored on every catalogue row. Two missions that today grade identically — the remote extrapolation and the roadside stronghold — are already distinguished by the catalogue; the grade simply does not read that column yet.

Potential issues

Everything above describes the pipeline as it runs today. This section is the other list: six places where a choice is worth revisiting, a specification and the code have drifted apart, or a number was calibrated on evidence thinner than it will eventually need. None of them is hidden — each is visible in the source, and each is quoted here with what it would take to settle it.

1 · There is no absolute test of whether a peak is real

The design calls the h-maxima floor required, not an optional prefilter, on the grounds that nothing else in the pipeline asks the question. It is switched off, for the measured reason given in B4 — the surrogate fit landed above the 90th percentile of real peaks — so detection rests on the fragment test and the cascade, both of which ask about scale rather than about height above noise. The cascade's own arithmetic puts the residual false-pass rate near 3% per level, which is a strong filter and not a proof. To settle: either fit the null on a surface model that is not itself a smoothed random field, or set H_MAXIMA_FIXED from the observed contrast distribution's bulk and measure what it removes.

2 · The draw exponent applies to the scale weight as well as to interest

The design writes the weight as interest⁴ × scale_weight; the code computes 4 · log(interest × scale_weight), which is (interest × scale_weight)⁴. The coarse-to-fine ratio at z6 is therefore 10¹²:1 rather than 1024:1. The map this produces is a reasonable one — see Part III for the measured level mix — but it is reasonable by cancellation between two large numbers rather than by the balance the design set out, and the two diverge if either the ladder or the exponent is ever changed. To settle: decide which form is wanted, then re-measure the level mix and the median interest of what a viewport shows.

3 · The grade runs on two of its three factors

Unrecorded nearby is specified, labelled in every translation file, and computed on every catalogue row as stronghold — but not read by the grade. Its absence is not neutral: with Rare worldwide saturated at zero for 60% of rows, the grade is effectively one-dimensional for the median mission, and the remote extrapolation grades the same as the roadside stronghold. To settle: adding it moves every cut-off in the tier table, so it needs the re-measurement the v2 tiers already got.

4 · The grade tiers under-deliver their intended shares

The cut-offs were chosen to restore roughly the top 5 / 10 / 35%. Measured on Thun they give 3.1 / 6.9 / 22.9%, and they were calibrated on that one region. To settle: re-measure the score distribution on the national build before treating the tiers as final.

5 · Prominence is absent from the shipped catalogue

PROMINENCE_ENABLED is off because the union-find pass is the build's CPU bottleneck at ~1,000 species, and prominence is not scored. The cost is diagnostic rather than functional: saddle_p — the field that says whether a peak merged over a real col or through the 0-fill, and so whether a candidate is a fragment — is null everywhere, which is exactly the reasoning B6 wanted kept auditable. To settle: run it once per region rather than never, or compute saddle_p alone.

6 · The regional builder approximates the domain mask

B3's three states are meant to come from the model's published footprint polygon. The regional builder infers them from tile responses instead: served-but-empty is read as censored, a failed fetch as absent. Inland the two agree almost everywhere; they diverge precisely at the region's edges, which is where the apron exists to protect the statistics. To settle: fetch each species' footprint at build time, or measure how many candidates carry a valid_frac_2sigma low enough for the distinction to matter.
